Product Management Case Study

The Smooth Applicator

Oct 12, 2026 · @Sanjana

Summary

The Smooth Applicator is a one-handed, tremor-stabilizing, refillable lipstick. I chose to license the stabilization IP to established beauty companies instead of launching a D2C brand, and to test engineering feasibility before spending on consumer research. The $30 price is anchored to competitor refills and luxury tiers.

1 Discovery: the "why"

Applying lipstick on the go or during commutes often leads to awkward angles, missed edges and smudging. Existing portable and one-handed options, such as short balms or magnetic caps, have no stabilization features. That leaves aging consumers and people with hand tremors underserved. Traditional cosmetics also create single-use plastic waste, which drives demand for refillable packaging.

The global beauty and personal care market is projected to reach USD 534 billion, with U.S. cosmetics at USD 17.17 billion. Revlon, L'Oréal and Maybelline dominate, so capital barriers to entry are high for new direct-to-consumer brands. Refillable lipsticks today range from $20 (Fenty Icon The Fill) to $199 (Valé Armor), and none integrate stabilization hardware.

2 Strategy: where and how to play

The decision was to prioritize a B2B intellectual property and design licensing model over a standalone D2C brand. The aim is to engineer, patent and validate the stabilization mechanism, then pitch or license it to beauty conglomerates that already own global supply chains.

3 Product requirements: the "what"

Core benefit proposition: effortless, on-the-go application powered by a magnetic one-handed design and stabilization technology, with flexible, sustainable shade refills.

  • One-handed form factor: a magnetic closure cap with a top assist knob for unassisted opening.
  • Multi-stage sliding mechanism: a side slide with three locks. The bottom lock secures the lipstick when not in use, the middle lock positions it for standard application, and the top lock unlocks the internal spindle so empty wax refills can be replaced quickly.
  • Stabilization device: a permanent component in the slide mechanism using mechanical or electronic tremor reduction, adapted from medical assistive products such as Liftware Steady and the GYENNO Spoon.
  • Sustainable materials: interchangeable organic wax inserts with lanolin oil, carnauba wax and beeswax, in a durable finished steel or lacquered metal shell.

4 Prototyping and technical feasibility: the "build"

Today the project has only conceptual 3D renderings and preliminary physical mock-ups, so engineering feasibility is unproven. The main risk is whether miniature gyroscopic or electronic stabilization can fit inside a compact cosmetic tube without making it too heavy or cost-prohibitive.

Validation plan

  1. Build high-fidelity CAD models with precise dimensions to test ergonomics.
  2. Build functional mechanical prototypes of the sliding mechanism and magnetic cap.
  3. Run structural stress tests of open/close durability and measure tremor-reduction efficiency.

6 Launch plan and roadmap: the "go-live"

  1. Feasibility and prototyping gate: finalize engineering blueprints and test whether the stabilization hardware can be manufactured cost-effectively.
  2. Qualitative and quantitative testing: run focus groups and interviews with industry professionals, everyday consumers and accessibility groups to measure real demand.
  3. Decision gate: choose between the two paths below.
  4. Product line expansion: if adoption succeeds, extend the form factor to adjacent products such as mascara wands or brow grooming tools.

7 Pricing: Economic Value Estimation

The Smooth Applicator is priced at $30, a $10 premium over basic refills that covers the magnetic mechanism, finished steel or lacquered metal housing and the embedded stabilization device.

Material cost is the main sensitivity. Standard finished steel keeps manufacturing in mid-tier ranges, while gold or silver plating would push the price toward the luxury end, at $50 or more.

8 What this case shows

  • Prioritizing by capital risk: I chose IP licensing over a D2C launch because incumbents control distribution and a new brand would carry hardware, inventory and marketing risk at once.
  • Testing the riskiest assumption first: the roadmap gates on engineering feasibility before consumer demand testing, with a fallback (Path A) if the technology fails.
  • Anchoring price in value: the $30 price is built from competitor benchmarks and a clear $10 premium, and willingness to pay is a defined validation metric.
